# Costa Rican Healthcare Sector Debt Reform

## Introduction
The healthcare system of Costa Rica is composed of a multitude of agencies within the Costa Rican State. Some of these institutions are autonomous, and bureaucratically independent from the Costa Rican President or Congress, while others are directly controlled by the Executive Power. Throughout this paper, we will first discuss the inception of the Costa Rican Social Security Fund–the most important institution that this sector has–the debates and reforms which impacted it, and its interaction with other parts of the Health sector such as the Ministry of Health, the Ministry of Labor and Social Security, the Medics Union, FODESAF, etc. This history will also include analysis offered by reformers of the time in addition to my own, the analysis of Panamá’s own CSS and Ministry of Health, and how failed reforms failed reforms can warn about future dangers to Costa Rica. Finally, a discussion of possible reforms to these issues, that fit the status quo of the system, will be presented with the goal of strengthening the institution that has cared for millions of Costa Ricans since its birth more than 84 years ago.

## Present Situation (1986-)
Since the end of President Monge Alvarez’s administration, continued weakenings of the system have been institutionalized through impositions of different International Financial Institutions (IFIs) such as the IMF, IDB, World Bank (WB), etc.; pressure from business groups, or through avoidance of duties. The tax reforms enacted from impositions of the aforementioned IFIs have lowered tax rates for the wealthiest, created uneven taxation rates that unequally affect the working class, and weakened autonomous governmental institutions by forcing them to pay income taxes–inducing privatization (Ulate 2000). These changes have continued to pressure the healthcare system, and have required additional measures meant to solidify their finances. Reforms have largely refused to tax the wealthiest sectors of Costa Rican society, but have resulted in increases for the working class. The system of Social Charges has only continued to endure increased evasion of payments, from both the private sector and, most importantly, the central government. To illustrate this, total debt from independent workers and employers to the CCSS grew from ₡59.969 billion CRC in 2012, to ₡99.963 billion CRC in 2015. Most of this tax evasion stems from the 8% growth of informal employment between those years, since they account for ₡52 billion CRC of the 2015 total number, more than double of their 2012 figure (Arias et al. 2016). For the government, in 2011, it owed the CCSS ₡421.276 billion CRC, with 75% of it having no formalized payment scheme to relieve the CCSS (Badilla & Nazareth 2018).
	
The increase in this debt has only worsened as the mechanisms used in the past by the Executive power have remained unchecked. As discussed in the prior section, the government continues finding different ways to circumvent limits on the investment of IVM funds, an example of this kind of behavior is the use of government bonds as payment for debts with the SEM, now renamed Health Insurance (RS), system. Because the RS needs to use revenue to pay immediate needs of its insured population, the CCSS has has resorted to the use of IVM reserves to buy these bonds, as a way to transfer liquidity to the RS–a practice that has worsened since the lowering of IVM reserve requirements in 1986-1990 under the presidency of Oscar Arias Sanchez. The liquidity issues are worsened by cash heavy labor payouts, such as the payment of full wages for sick days, layoff (firing, retirement, or death, in which case the family of the deceased receives the payment) payments of up to 20 consecutive years, and improper tracking of the number of hours worked by employees (Badilla & Nazareth 2018) & (Rodriguez 2009).

As of December 2024, audits conducted by the General Comptroller of the Republic (CGR) revealed the total debt of the State of Costa Rica to the CCSS had reached nearly ₡4.2 Trillion CRC ($8.4 billion USD), approximately 8.8% of Costa Rica’s total GDP for 2024. More specifically, the issue has been made worse by the lack of consistent accounting between the CCSS, the Executive Power, and FODESAF, which disagree on the amount owed by the State to the Social Security Fund. Per the CGR, the Ministry of Finance and FODESAF only recognize approximately ₡951 billion CRC of the total owed to the Fund–₡765 billion CRC from medical services corresponding to FODESAF, and ₡186 billion CRC directly owed by the Executive Power (El Observador 2025). The accounting from the Central Government raises concerns, due to its failure to prevent double counting the transactions between different institutions within its control, failing to meet the standards set by the International Public Sector Accounting Standards Board (IPSASB), of which Costa Rica is a signatory (CGR 2025).

## Future Recommendations

From the Panamá system, it is clear that too many regulations, on the investment of these funds, might lead to the system’s demise. While the costs incurred by the Panamanian system in 2005 to create the new model did have some effect on its fate–it is more probable that the impositions made by the IMF were the cause. The limits made it so that Panamá (as of 2019) had to place more than 60% of its investment funds in savings accounts at banks, limiting their ability to diversify their investments, and increasing the State’s dependence on IFI loan disbursements. Costa Rica also has an issue of diversification of investment too–but not in the same type of investment–with more than 60% used to hold debt of other public institutions of Costa Rica (Mesa-Lago 2021). The current state of the IVM system opens up the possibility for pensions to lose a massive amount of value, similar to the way in which it happened under President Carazo Odio, if any of the other autonomous institutions of the State faced liquidity issues. The diversification of investment into initiatives for more equitable development, such as affordable housing, public transit, etc., should be pursued by the institution to avoid the homogeneity in investment that currently exists. These investments could also be tied to an individual's affiliation to the institution (in terms of housing), and it would also be a possibility to make use of the socio-economic studies that the Fund undertakes when analyzing prospective enrollments. For public transit, it had already been done previously in the early 1960s–when the Fund lent to the nationalized pacific railway for purchases of new trains (CCSS 1962). This would also offer the chance to the Costa Rican government to avoid undertaking more expensive loans from IFIs, by opening the possibility to reduce the amount the State loaned from them for important development projects.

Although the diversification of the reserves is an exciting prospect, especially for a nation that needs to continue developing its infrastructure and reducing economic inequalities across the entire national territory, the Costa Rican government needs to resolve its differences in accounting across institutions. To solve this, a joint debt-repayment roadmap between the Ministry of Finance, FODESAF, and the CCSS–with the technical assistance of the CGR–needs to be created. As pointed out before, the CGR has discovered many differences in accounting, duplicate sums of revenue, and improper valuations of assets, which would also avoid wasting more time, and resources, on duplicate audits if done jointly (CGR 2025). The increased transparency in accounting would consequently allow the CCSS to make use of legal measures, to ensure the government remains accountable to its finances. Additionally, the processing of File 24.02523–a bill, not yet voted or debated, that aims to eliminate the position of the Executive Presidency of the CCSS–should be prioritized by Congress and the Executive Power24. The elimination of this role needs to be a priority, since this role has allowed Presidents of the Republic to increase control over the autonomous institutions of the State, violating their constitutional mandate (Delfino 2025a). The additional control of the Executive has resulted in the CCSS to be the largest debt holder of the State, and even allowing the State to pay the spending (that it chooses to recognize) with bonds or other assets of value lesser than direct transactions (Badilla & Nazareth 2018). 

The restructuring of the Board of Directors of the CCSS, contained within File 24.025, which reforms its nine seats to be equally distributed for elected representatives of workers, employers, and the State, would democratize the decisionmaking of the institution (Delfino 2025a). In this way, welfare-reducing decisions that add unnecessary costs to the institution, such as the construction delays of the Dr. Maximiliano Peralta Jimenez Hospital in Cartago25–an effort led by Marta Esquivel Rodriguez, Executive President of the CCSS–can be avoided in the future (Diario Extra 2025). Another way in which this reform helps the institution by the added representation of the labor sector, and the reduced interference of the government, is that workers now get to be directly involved in ways to better service, and would force the government to fulfill its constitutional mandate as the protector of the working class of the country. To combat tax evasion from non-government actors, the reform of evasion incentivizing laws such as those regarding banking confidentiality, should also be prioritized, expanding upon those proposed by File 24.65826, which reforms the limitations set to obtain financial records of organized crime beneficiaries (Delfino 2025b). The added measures should focus on financial crimes, and allow the CCSS to prohibit or severely restrict individuals, who owe large debts to the Fund, from accessing more debt without scheduling repayments to their previous financial responsibilities. This reform would force employers that abuse LLC structures to avoid paying the institution, an example of this being the largest corporate tax-evader of the CCSS, which owed (as of 2018), more than ₡2 billion CRC to the institution, but was not forced to pay since current laws only allow the fund to close companies that do not pay. Using the proposed reforms to investments, banking, institutions, and debt repayment, the State can address the root cause of the sector’s financial woes, so that it can continue protecting and serving all Costa Rican citizens.
